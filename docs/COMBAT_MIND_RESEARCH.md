# Combat minds: what is known

Written 2026-11-11 on the Spark (branch `design-combat-mind`), as the research half of [COMBAT_MIND.md](COMBAT_MIND.md). This is a survey kept to what this project can use, not a literature review. No model was called and nothing was run. Sources were read through web search and, where marked, their abstract or primary page. Most game-industry talks were read through summaries, because the slides and videos are behind GDC Vault or not online.

**Evidence levels used below.**

- **Established practice:** shipped in several games and taught in the field's standard texts; several independent sources agree.
- **One source's claim:** a single paper, talk or vendor page says it. Not replicated that I could find.
- **Vendor claim:** the maker of a product says it about its own product.
- **Could not verify:** only secondary or community sources were found, or the primary text was not reachable.
- **This project's evidence:** measured here, with the sample size given where the record has it.

## 1. How real-time combat AI is built in games

### Behaviour trees, planners and utility

| Technique | What it is | Evidence | Use here |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Behaviour trees** | Prioritized, reactive selection among behaviours, re-evaluated often. Halo 2's AI was "a behavior DAG" of about 50 behaviours with a prioritized-list decision scheme; event handling was weak, and Bungie added "impulses" for alternative triggers ([Isla, GDC 2005](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/gdc-2005-proceeding-handling-complexity-in-the-i-halo-2-i-ai)). | Established practice (Halo 2 is the usual origin; the [Wikipedia survey](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavior_tree_(artificial_intelligence,_robotics_and_control)) and many engines follow). Halo 2 details: one source's claim, read through summaries. | This engine's behaviour graph is one (`first`, `seq`, `if`, `repeat`, `desires`). It is the right execution layer. |
| **GOAP** | A planner searches over actions with preconditions and effects; F.E.A.R.'s state machine had three states (Goto, Animate, UseSmartObject) and A* planned action sequences as well as paths ([Orkin, GDC 2006](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~dyer/cs540/handouts/gdc2006_orkin_jeff_fear.pdf)). | Established practice (primary paper read). | Not needed as a runtime planner. Its lesson is the split: a small set of execution states, and planning above them. |
| **HTN planning** | Abstract tasks decomposed into concrete actions, with backtracking. Killzone 2 used it for soldiers and multiplayer bots ([Verweij thesis](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/VUA07_Verweij_Hierarchically-Layered-MP-Bot_System.pdf)); Horizon Zero Dawn's machines too ([Guerrilla, The AI of HZD](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/The-AI-of-Horizon-Zero-Dawn.pdf), [HTN planning in Decima](https://guerrilla-games.com/read/htn-planning-in-decima)). | Established practice. | A scheme (a routine the mind writes, COMBAT_MIND §3.8) is close to an HTN method: a named way of doing a task, with the conditions under which it applies. |
| **Utility systems** | Every option gets a score from curves over considerations; the best scoring option runs. Dave Mark's Infinite Axis Utility System ([gameai.com](https://gameai.com/iaus.php)) is the best-known form. | Established practice as a family. IAUS's details: could not verify (talks are on GDC Vault). | The graph's `desires` top level is a weighted chooser already. Scheme selection could be scored the same way when no decision model answers. |

### Position, space and timing

- **Tactical position evaluation.** Killzone scored candidate positions near a soldier with a weighted sum of factors borrowed from chess (mobility, board control, king safety), and used the same factors to cost paths, because "shortest paths often aren't tactically sound" ([Straatman & van der Sterren](https://www.cp.eng.chula.ac.th/~vishnu/gameResearch/AI/KillzoneAI_Worapoj.pdf), [GDC Europe 2005 slides](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/gdce05_killzone_ai.pdf)). *Established practice* (the slides are primary). Here: a movement intent such as "hold range" or "break line of sight" picks its spot by scoring nearby points, in code, on the authority.
- **Influence maps.** Layered grids of influence (threat, presence, cover) combined to answer "where": "the influence map doesn't actually provide instruction—it only provides information that is used by the decision-making structures" ([Mark, Game AI Pro 2, ch. 30](https://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter30_Modular_Tactical_Influence_Maps.pdf)). *Established practice.* Here: for one or two opponents a point score is enough; an influence layer matters for group combat, later.
- **Taking turns to attack.** DOOM (2016) is widely reported to limit how many demons attack at once through a shared pool of attack tokens ([summary of the talk](https://serfofcinder.itch.io/archetype-portal-to-hell/devlog/738495/enemy-ai-tech-talk)). *Could not verify* against the talk itself. It is a fairness device decided by the designer for the player's benefit. **It must not be copied here**: it is a guard on what characters decide.
- **Telegraphs and reaction windows.** Action games give attacks a readable windup so the player can answer. Community analyses put a simple visual reaction at about 250–265 ms (15–16 frames at 60 Hz) and a choice between answers at about 380 ms; with a familiar telegraph, players picked the right answer about 74% of the time at 26–30 frames (430–500 ms) and over 90% at 30+ frames in an informal Virtua Fighter test ([Infil, On reaction times](https://ki.infil.net/reaction.html), [Virtua Fighter forum test](https://host.virtuafighter.com/threads/reaction-time-tests-on-different-games-and-setups.22576)). A commonly repeated rule that a telegraph under about 340 ms frustrates players traces to a forum post. *Could not verify* any of these against peer-reviewed measurement; the orders of magnitude agree across sources. **Use here:** today's strike windup is 350 ms, the bow shot 300 ms, the fire bolt 450 ms, the heal 600 ms (`skills.rhai` `combat_*`). A person can just react to a seen strike and comfortably to a fire bolt. A model that answers in 150 ms *plus* transport cannot react to a strike at all (COMBAT_MIND §2.4).
- **Learnable boss patterns.** Souls-like bosses draw from a pool of five or more attacks with conditions rather than a fixed sequence, so players learn the pool and its conditions ([Bycer, Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/the-impact-of-dark-souls-on-boss-design)). Delayed attacks are a deliberate feint and feel unfair when the telegraph is too subtle ([Wayline](https://www.wayline.io/blog/the-art-of-the-fakeout-combat-design)). *Could not verify* beyond designers' commentary. **Use here:** the arena's scripted opponents should be pools with conditions, not fixed loops, or a mind will learn a loop and nothing transferable.

### Fighting-game AI

- **Search with an opponent model.** In the FightingICE competition, plain MCTS assumes a random opponent; Kim & Kim added an action table updated during the game to capture the opponent's patterns ([2017](https://cilab.gist.ac.kr/hp/wp-content/uploads/publications/international_conference/2017/opponent_modeling_based_on_action_table_for_mcts-based_fighting_game_ai.pdf)). A rolling-horizon evolutionary bot with a learned opponent model placed second in 2019 ([arXiv 2003.13949](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.13949)). *One source's claim* each. **Use here:** an opponent table updated per exchange is the cheapest within-fight learner there is. Under the owner's rule (COMBAT_MIND, top) the engine builds no such table: a character's picture of an opponent is what its own mind notices and remembers (COMBAT_MIND §3.8).
- **N-gram prediction.** Keep the frequencies of the opponent's next move given its last *n*; long associated with melee combo games, and known to make "unbeatable (no fun)" opponents unless weakened ([Georgia Tech lecture, after Millington's *AI for Games*](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~surban6/2019fa-gameAI/lectures/2019_11_18-Prediction_Ngram.pdf)). *Established practice* as a technique. **Use here:** a counter of "what X did after I closed in / after its heavy blow" is a 1-gram over situations; enough to start with. It must be built from perceived events only.
- **Learning a player across matches: Killer Instinct's Shadows.** A Shadow records every action of every match its player fights, extracts 400–700 patterns per match, needs three matches for base competence, and keeps up to 40 matches per matchup ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/the-killer-groove-the-shadow-ai-of-killer-instinct), [GDC 2016 video summary](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/video-designing-competitive-game-ai-the-i-killer-instinct-i-way)). *One source's claim* (the developers'); the model itself is not described publicly. **Use here:** evidence that a per-opponent store of situational patterns, kept across fights, reads as a person to players. Here it is a character's personal memory of a person, not a profile the engine keeps.

## 2. Language models acting in real-time games

### The latency gap

- **Speed beats depth in fast games, up to a point.** On a Street Fighter benchmark, lowering latency improved win rate until quality fell too far; the authors' method picks model size and quantization per moment and reports win-rate gains "up to 80%" ([Kang et al., NeurIPS 2025, arXiv 2505.19481](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19481)). The often-quoted "20% faster wins consistently" is in secondary summaries, not the abstract. *One source's claim.* The open [llm-colosseum](https://github.com/OpenGenerativeAI/llm-colosseum) benchmark says its rankings depend heavily on API latency. *Could not verify* beyond its README.
- **Summaries over raw frames.** TextStarCraft II turns game state into text, and "chain of summarization" condenses single frames and then several frames before deciding; most of 10 tested LLMs beat the built-in AI at level 5 ([Ma et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/f0ebc318e2df08360b2df559e81602e5-Abstract.html)). *One source's claim.* StarCraft's strategic layer tolerates seconds of latency; a duel's does not.
- **This project's evidence.** Measured here ([MODEL_COST.md](MODEL_COST.md), [MIND_HARNESS.md](MIND_HARNESS.md)): a full deliberation (think then compile) is 15–30 s p50; DeepSeek V4.1 Flash compiles at 3.8 s p50 in a good hour and 10–11 s in a loaded one; talk-sized calls are about 1.8 s; Jev judgments 136–174 ms p50 and 166–240 ms p95 from the dev-box. In 25 measured wolf fights, exchanges lasted one blow at p50 and 19 s at p90 (COMBAT_MIND §2.1). So **a large model is at most a between-exchanges reviser in a duel, and often too late even for that.**

### Models that write the policy that runs without them

- **Code as policy.** Code-writing LLMs can write robot policy code that parameterizes control primitives, including reactive controllers ([Code as Policies, arXiv 2209.07753](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.07753)). *One source's claim*, widely built on.
- **A skill library that grows.** Voyager writes executable skills, verifies them against the environment, stores them in a library for retrieval, and reports 3.3× more unique items and tech milestones up to 15.3× faster than earlier agents ([Wang et al., arXiv 2305.16291](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16291)). *One source's claim.* **Use here:** schemes as a library the mind keeps, verified in use (their outcomes), retrieved by situation.
- **LLM-written behaviour trees for game agents.** Ito & Takahashi extend behaviour trees with a "behavior branch" that an LLM (Code Llama 34B) writes at runtime from players' free-text commands, loaded into a Unity game ([arXiv 2402.07442](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07442)). PORTAL has an LLM write behaviour trees in a DSL for thousands of FPS games, policies "instantaneously deployable"; its abstract gives no numbers and does not say generation is offline ([arXiv 2503.13356](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13356)). Adaptive Command pairs an LLM advisor with a behaviour tree that executes, in StarCraft II, and reports user-study gains without figures in the abstract ([arXiv 2508.16580](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.16580)). *One source's claim* each. This project already does this: minds write their graphs and patch the `combat` branch mid-fight.
- **Evolving the written code.** Eureka samples reward functions from an LLM, scores them by training outcomes and refines them, beating human-written rewards on 83% of 29 tasks ([Ma et al., ICLR 2024, arXiv 2310.12931](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12931)). *One source's claim.* **Use here:** the owner's "evolve its architecture in arenas" has this shape: generate scheme variants, score them by arena outcomes, keep the better. It belongs in the lab, as a way to find which prompts and scheme forms work, never as a way to hand characters tactics.

### Fast and slow parts

- **SwiftSage**: a small fine-tuned model (T5-large, 770M) acts; a large model takes over when the small one is stuck (5 steps without reward) ([NeurIPS 2023, arXiv 2305.17390](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17390)). *One source's claim.*
- **Talker–Reasoner**: a fast "talker" and a slow "reasoner" share memory; the reasoner writes beliefs, the talker reads them, and may wait for it ([Christakopoulou et al., arXiv 2410.08328](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08328)). *One source's claim*, shown on a coaching agent, not a game.
- **Small typed decision models beside large ones.** TypeSafe's Jev answers several typed questions (yes/no probability, choice, score) against one shared state; their Doom demo ran about 10 queries a second with 70–500 ms end-to-end latency, and they say a conventional bot could play better ([launch post](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev), summarized in [JEV_BEHAVIOR_RESEARCH.md](JEV_BEHAVIOR_RESEARCH.md)). *Vendor claim.* Its docs say it "is not a calculator", "does not count reliably" and reads dates as text ([model jaggedness](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness.md)), so counts, distances and times must be turned into words or bands in code. *Vendor claim*, matching how this project already builds its rethink state.
- **This project's evidence.** The `judged` condition works as built: a cached `fight_risk` rose with wounds and the graph reacted within one tick, at a fraction of a cent a judgment. It changed outcomes for the worse, because the graph's answer (flee on foot) was wrong, not the judgment (n = 3 per arm; [MIND_HARNESS.md](MIND_HARNESS.md) item 4). The owner decided on 2026-11-09 that Jev "is not real-time" and never drives moment-to-moment choices.

## 3. Noticing an opponent's patterns and answering them

- **Within a fight.** The game-AI answer is an opponent table updated per action (Kim & Kim above; n-grams). The LLM-agent answer is text feedback after each turn: PokéLLMon refines its choices within a battle from the environment's text feedback, adds a "Pokédex" of game facts so it does not invent them, and reports a 49% ladder win rate; it also names "panic switching", erratic changes of plan against a strong opponent ([Hu et al., arXiv 2402.01118](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01118)). *One source's claim.* Both lessons carry over: give the mind the facts of its own skills as numbers, and expect plan thrash under pressure (the engine already reports "turning back and forth between two targets").
- **Across fights.** Reflexion keeps a written reflection after each failed attempt and uses it in the next ([Shinn et al., NeurIPS 2023, arXiv 2303.11366](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366)). *One source's claim.* This project's consolidation and sleep already turn experiences into beliefs; a fight's lessons can be held the same way.
- **Finding weaknesses on purpose.** AlphaStar's league trained "exploiter" agents whose only job was to expose the main agents' weaknesses, reset periodically for diversity ([DeepMind, 2019](https://deepmind.google/blog/alphastar-grandmaster-level-in-starcraft-ii-using-multi-agent-reinforcement-learning/)); later work reports the exploiters lose that ability late in training ([NeurIPS 2023](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2023/poster/70220)). *One source's claim* each. **Use here:** the arena's scripted opponents are exploiters by design: each one is built to punish one habit.

## 4. Reading as a person playing, not a script

- **Turing tests for bots.** The BotPrize ran human judges against bots in Unreal Tournament 2004; in 2008 and 2009 no bot fooled most judges ([Hingston](https://www.technologyreview.com/2009/09/10/93435/a-turing-test-for-computer-game-bots/), [Wikipedia](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_game_bot_Turing_test)). In 2012 UT^2 and MirrorBot were each judged human 52% of the time ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2012/09/27/game_bot_turing_test/)). *Established* as a result series.
- **What made UT^2 read as human.** Evolved combat skill, *filtered* so it stayed humanlike (an optimized bot evolves perfect aim, which reads as a bot), plus replay of recorded human movement traces ([Schrum, Karpov & Miikkulainen, CIG 2011](https://nn.cs.utexas.edu/downloads/papers/schrum.cig11competition.pdf)). *One source's claim.* A shooter study reported second-hand that decision time and aiming skill are the critical parameters for humanlike bots ([Glavin & Madden, arXiv 1806.05554](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.05554)); *could not verify* the original. **Use here:** reaction times far under a person's and perfect timing will read as a script, even when the decisions are good. The engine must not add an artificial delay for AI (it would be a rule that differs by controller); a mind that answers through a 150 ms model and a 67 ms tick cadence has human-scale delays by construction.
- **Learned judges of human-likeness.** Microsoft's Navigation Turing Test trained a model to predict human judges' verdicts on human-likeness; it agreed well with them, but "perform[s] poorly when asked to differentiate between different types of artificial agents" ([Devlin et al., ICML 2021](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/devlin21a.html)). *One source's claim.* **Use here:** an automatic "reads as a player" score can screen, not decide; the owner's ratings stay the reference (as [TRIALS.md](TRIALS.md) already holds for the feel score).
- **Believability by interview and ablation.** Generative Agents measured believability by interviewing agents and ablating memory, reflection and planning; each contributed ([Park et al., UIST 2023, arXiv 2304.03442](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03442)). *One source's claim.* **Use here:** after a fight, ask the character what it noticed about its opponent and check that against what the opponent actually showed (COMBAT_MIND §4.2).

## 5. What this project takes

1. **Execution is a reactive tree; deciding is above it.** Every shipped approach separates a fast executor from a slower chooser. Established practice.
2. **Prepared methods with applicability conditions** (HTN methods, Voyager's skills, the owner's "schemes") are how slow reasoning reaches fast execution without waiting. Established in games; one paper each for LLMs.
3. **An opponent table built per exchange is the cheapest within-fight learner.** Established technique; here it must be perception-bound and personal.
4. **A small typed model can choose but not react.** At 150–240 ms plus transport it is outside a 350 ms strike windup. This project's measurement plus the vendor's own limits.
5. **Numbers go to models as words and bands, computed in code.** Vendor claim plus this project's practice.
6. **Arena opponents should be exploiters with condition pools, not loops.** Analogy to AlphaStar's league and Souls design; not established for this use.
7. **Human-likeness is judged by people; automatic judges screen.** Established for bots (BotPrize); one paper for learned judges.
8. **No source found** reports an LLM mind that learns an opponent's patterns within a single real-time melee fight and changes approach before the fight ends. The nearest are turn-based (PokéLLMon) or strategic-tempo (StarCraft). *Could not verify* that nothing exists; the owner's sense that this is novel is consistent with what search found.
