# Why the fast lane disengages

**Recommendation (a). Test a balanced frame first. Keep the fast lane off.** The battle evidence supports a concrete mechanism: urgent threat cues go into a full-branch rewrite, and many replies give withdrawal priority over the existing fight plan. It cannot isolate how much comes from the prompt versus the survival incentives. A small framing trial is the most direct way to distinguish them before changing combat rules.

Across B1, B2, B100 and C2, **845 of 1,157 installed replies (73%) contained `flee` or `back_off`** in the replacement graph or selected routine. **351 (30%) contained a withdrawal action with no enclosing condition**, and only **64 (6%)** used an explicit approach or an attack tied to an opening or opponent health. Most still contained an attack action (1,004, 87%). The order matters. A higher-priority move can take the fighter out of range before the attack below it can run. The first comparable self-heal cutoff fell in 223 replies and rose in 38. The withdrawal pattern extends beyond toggling heal.

In B2, all 1,505 observed retreat starts followed an install on the same fighter, and 798 returned to contact within ten seconds. B2 spent 60.0% of sampled fighter-time in contact versus 97.5% in A2. B100 replicated the contact loss (43.3% versus 93.5%), with no aimed blows observed from minute six. B2 met 70.4% of sampled incoming blows with timely defence after install. That measure is conditional on being attacked and gives no credit or penalty for how often the fighter stays in a fight. C2, with half the B2 lane caps, still installed 202 replies and had 78.2% contact.

**The frame gives an urgent event a durable answer.** The logged system prompt leads with the incident and offers combat rewrite, routine rewrite, routine switch, then keep. It explicitly teaches that stepping back makes a blow miss and that a person who stops to swing can be outrun. Attack, approach and timed conditions are legal too, but the concrete combat recipe couples threat with escape, and the repertoire example explicitly encodes flight. A fighter can replace its existing attack with a one-node retreat or switch to “stay safe.” An equally short attack is legal. Writing a policy that takes a step back, waits for an opening and reliably returns to reach takes more work.

**The model receives little evidence for deciding whether to abandon the fight.** Every captured request says “(no persona yet).” These are Walker fixtures with forager routines and the same seeded fight desire. The prompt gives recent impressions and a hit or windup, without current HP, a current ally/enemy count or a live view of the threat. Retreat can be sensible in a lethal free-for-all with no stated allies, particularly after a hard hit. The observed cues do not consistently separate withdraw-only from offense-only edits. The model's explanation of being surrounded is a report from the model. It does not verify the threat state or identify what caused its choice.

**Opus build and small trial.** Give each legal option an equally short example and preserve the existing fight branch by default. Offer keep, adjust defence timing, change target, press an opening, make room and re-enter, and break contact. Make a local edit or a bounded spacing move with an explicit return condition as easy to express as flight. Preserve the option to leave and give it an explicit condition. State the existing fight goal and distinguish the triggering percept from current observations. In an authorized small trial, compare the old and balanced frames on the same saved inputs first, keeping model and rules fixed. Then compare matched small fights with the lane off, the original frame and the revised frame. Judge contact, aimed attacks, retreats and returns alongside timed defence and damage. Keep stronger world context or different combat incentives for separate comparisons so the framing change can be evaluated on its own.
