# Cullet: world bible

The source for the authored world `cullet` ([small city](../../../../docs/SMALL_CITY.md)). The residents' sheets are drafted from this text and the cast list (`cast.json`), then checked and compiled into `living/seeds/cullet.json`. Everything here is what is the case on the first morning. Nothing in it says what happens next: once the world runs, what people do is theirs.

## The world as its people know it

The world is one island, **the Wrack**, and the water round it. It is named after the seaweed the sea throws up on its beaches. It is about three-quarters of a kilometre from west to east, a rock with thin soil, short grass, heather and wind. Nobody alive has seen any other land.

- **Cullet** is the only town. It stands on the south slope, which faces the sun, and steps down to a stone harbour. Everyone in the world lives in it or within a few minutes' walk of it.
- **The Combs** are a reef 120 to 200 m off the south shore. The sea breaks white on them in any weather. Nothing with a keel has crossed them since the Last Sail.
- **The Pale** is a bank of fog that stands beyond the Combs on every side, all year, day and night. From the Crown you see the harbour, the Combs, a band of grey sea, then white. Nobody has been into it. It does not move.
- **The Lintel** stands on the north headland, 330 m from town: two standing stones and a capstone, with a slab of dark glass filling the opening. It is shut. Nobody knows who set it there or what it is for.

There are no boats that can cross the Combs, no animals but gulls, fish and Fenn's hens, and no wood but the Hanger. Nothing comes from outside. Whatever breaks is mended, remelted or reused.

### The count of years

Years are counted from **the Last Sail**, the day the last ship left the harbour. It was not seen again, and no ship has come in since. This is **year 83**. Nobody living saw the ships: the oldest person in Cullet, Halvard Dunmore, was born in year 13. What anyone knows of the ship days comes from parents, grandparents, the carved lintels and the ship-day tablets.

Three layers of the past are visible in the town, and people know them as they know their own houses:

1. **The ship days** (before year 0). Cullet was a port of call. Ships came, paid their dues at the Counting House and left. The harbour wall, the mole, the dry dock, the bonded warehouse and bollards far too big for a fishing boat are from then, and so are the carved lintels in a formal script. The Counting House clock stopped at ten past four. People say it stopped the day of the Last Sail; nobody knows.
2. **The lean years** (the first thirty years or so after the Last Sail, the elders' parents' time). When the ships stopped, half the town went with the last of them or died. The roofs of empty houses were stripped for fuel and the wood was cut down to stumps. Three houses in the Rows are still roofless shells. The Hanger was replanted about thirty years ago by Marit Sedge's mother, with Marit beside her as a girl, so its trees are all of an age and stand among old stumps.
3. **Now.** Glasshouses built inside the walls of old house plots. A band of newer, redder brick on the Cone where it cracked. **The storm of last autumn** broke three glasshouses, stripped slates off half the town and took the end rail of the mole. **The fever winter**, six years ago, killed nine people (below).

### The dead whom the living still name

The dead cannot be spoken to, and they are not in anyone's household. People remember them.

- **The fever winter** (six years ago) took Iona and Aurel Pontil (Lumi's parents; Iona was Vessa's only daughter), Arvo and Liss Dunmore (Tobiah's parents; Arvo was Halvard and Greta's son), Matthias Sarro (a blower, Runa's husband, Imre's father), Bett Brandt (Otto's sister, Juno's mother), Maddy Dunlin (Rafe's wife, Tilly's mother), Ada Frit (the mother of Tamsin Hollin and Suri Greave) and Siri Morrow (Ingrid's daughter, Lark's mother). Mirren Quill nursed most of them and was the only healer.
- **Sander Vane**, brother of Lorcan, Davin and Petra, drowned off the mole the winter before his son Wick was born. He and Thessaly Marl never married.
- Others died longer ago and more ordinarily: Vessa's husband, Magda Tarn's husband (lost at the Weirs), the Vane siblings' parents, Livia Faro's parents (Anselm raised her in the lamp house), Juno's father and Lark's father.

## The land and what it gives

Every material and every food in Cullet has a place it comes from, and you can walk to each of them. Walking times are short: the length of the quay in a minute, the Fish Steps to the Crown in a minute and a half, the Lintel in about four.

| Place | Where | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| **The Strand** | The quay along the harbour, the Fish Steps, the mole | Fish by line from the Fish Steps, the quay edge and the mole |
| **The Weirs** | Stake fences in the shallows south-west of town | Fish by trap, worked from the shallows |
| **The Wrack Strand** | The west beach | Kelp, thrown up by the sea: food for the root plots and ash for glass. The four round kelp kilns stand here, upwind of Low Row |
| **The Sand Spit** | The east shore | White sand for glass |
| **The root plots** | Eighteen walled plots west of and above the Ladder | Roots, the staple |
| **The Ladder** | Fourteen glasshouses on terraces, upper west side | Greens and a little fruit. Eleven are working; three have been broken since the storm |
| **The Moss** | The peat moor on the plateau north of town | Peat, the main fuel. The turf house and the peat barn stand at its edge |
| **The Hanger** | A wind-bent copse in a gully to the north-east, about seventy trees | The only wood |
| **The clay bank** | Below the Crown | Clay for the crucibles the glass melts in. It is nearly dug out |
| **The cistern** | On the Crown, 9 m across | Water, from rain |

### The food chain

People live by it and talk about it in these terms:

- **Fish** are most of what people eat: caught by line at dawn or in the weirs, eaten fresh, cooked, or **smoked** in the two smokehouses on the quay so they keep. Fresh fish spoil in about a day.
- **Roots** are the staple. They are grown in the walled plots, fed with kelp. People **bake their roots at the Batch**, the common oven on Low Row, in the morning while it is hot. A baked root is a meal; a raw one is half of one.
- **Greens** grow under glass on the Ladder, in any weather.
- **Kelp** is gathered on the Wrack Strand. Some is carried up the hill to the root plots. The rest is **burnt to ash** in the kelp kilns or any good fire: four loads of kelp make one of ash.
- **Glass** is ash and sand, one of ash to two of sand, melted at the Cone by someone who knows glassmaking. Every melt draws on the Cone's fire. Glass mends the glasshouses and the windows, and makes floats, bottles, lamp glass and drops.
- **Lamp oil** is rendered from fish over a fire: two fish make a measure of oil. The Watchglass burns it every night.
- **Peat** is cut on the Moss and carried down to the peat barn. It burns in every hearth, in the Batch, in the smokehouses and in the Cone. Wood from the Hanger burns too, and better, but there is very little of it.

A fire burns only while it is fed. A hearth left unfed goes out; a smokehouse or the Batch has to be fuelled before it can be used; the Cone eats more peat than all the hearths in town together and is tended in three watches so that it never goes out. Lamplight, warmth and cooking all come from fires that someone has fed.

### Fuel, the tight thing

Food is not short. The sea and the plots give more than the town eats if the food trades work their hours, and the Store on the quay holds several days of food. **Fuel is short.** On the first morning the peat barn and the Store hold enough peat for about twelve days of the Cone at full fire, and the three cutters bring down less than the town burns: about seven loads for every ten burnt. The Hanger could make up the difference for a while. Marit Sedge, its warden, lets nobody fell a tree there. Everyone who keeps a hearth knows that the stack is lower each week.

## The town

The built town is about 300 m from east to west and climbs 20 m from the quay to the Crown. It is made of streets, not scattered houses: three terraced **Rows** along the slope, joined by five stair lanes called **wynds**.

| District | What is there |
|---|---|
| **The Strand** | The quay, the Fish Steps, **the Heron** (a tarred treadwheel crane from the ship days that still lifts boats and kelp), **the Counting House** with its yard (the only square, and the market), six tall black net lofts, two smokehouses, the slip, the boatshed, **the dry dock** with a half-built ship, and **the Store**, the common store |
| **Cinder Row** | **The Cone** and its yard, the lehr house (the old bonded warehouse), the pot house, the cullet shed with its heaps of coloured broken glass, the ash house, and the **wash-house** built against the Cone's warm north wall |
| **The mole** | The stone arm round the harbour, and at its tip **the Watchglass**, the harbour light, with the lamp house where the Faros live |
| **The Rows** | Low Row (Strand houses, and **the Batch**), Mid Row (mixed, and **the Marver**, the cookshop, with a terrace over the harbour), High Row (Kiln houses). Twenty-four houses, seventeen lived in, four sound and empty, three roofless |
| **The Ladder** | The glasshouses, the root plots, rain butts, kelp heaps |
| **The Crown** | The cistern, the Readers' House, the graveyard with its glass markers, one wind-bent thorn tree and a bench with the whole town below |
| **Outside town** | The turf house on the Moss (the Roons), the Hanger cottage (Marit Sedge), the peat barn, the Lintel |

**The landmarks** can be seen from most of the town, because it climbs a slope: the Cone, a 24 m brick cone that glows orange at its three mouths all night; the Watchglass, lit every night; the Counting House, with its arcade of seven arches and its stopped clock; the Heron's long black jib; and the Ladder's sawtooth of small panes, which lights up like lanterns at dusk.

**Glass in every window.** Nobody in Cullet can make a large clear sheet. Every pane is small, no more than about a forearm across, and windows are patched from bottle-green, amber and pale aqua panes. Cobalt is rare: the Watchglass has one band of it.

**The empty houses.** Low Row 3 is sound but damp to the sills and stands in the kelp smoke. High Row 4 and High Row 6 are sound; by custom High Row houses go to Kiln households, and both last belonged to Kiln families who died out. Mid Row 6 is sound and dry and nobody has a claim on it; its key hangs in the Counting House. Low Row 9, Mid Row 3 and High Row 7 are roofless shells. Gwil Stannard takes slates from the shells to patch other roofs, which is why every roof in town is several shades of grey.

## The people

Sixty-four people live in Cullet, in twenty-two households: fifty adults, six of them elders, and fourteen children. Everyone knows everyone by name and by face. A stranger has not been seen in living memory.

### Two kinds of family

- **Kiln families** descend from the glassmakers who were here in the ship days. They hold the Cone, the best houses on High Row and most shares in the Ladder: the Pontils, Hollins, Sarros, Quills, Brandts, Dunmores and Thessaly Marl. Their names are old Cullet names, several of them glassmakers' words.
- **Strand families** descend from the crews of the last ships to come in, which never went out again: some broke on the Combs trying, the rest rotted at the quay. Their crews stayed because they had to. They fish, gather wrack and live on Low Row: the Calloways, Tarns, Okoros, Vanes, Morrows, Satos and Dunlins. Their names come from every port those ships had touched, which is why Cullet's names are so mixed.
- **The others** are neither, or both: the growers of the Ladder (the Greaves, Fenwicks and Stannards), the Roons of the Moss, Marit Sedge of the Hanger, the Faros of the lamp house, the Achterbergs of the Marver and Benedikt Orme of the Readers' House.

Neither kind of family is a party or a rule. It is a past people carry: who your grandparents were, which row you grew up on, which trades your family has always had. There are marriages, debts and friendships across the line. Runa Sarro of the lehr was born a Tarn. Tamsin Hollin and her sister Suri Greave were born Frits, a Kiln family that has no one else left. Thessaly Marl's son Wick is a Vane on his father's side.

### Who does what

Cullet has no head and no council. Things are decided by whoever holds the thing in question, and argued about under the arches of the Counting House. People know who holds what:

- **The Glassmaster** (Vessa Pontil) decides everything at the Cone: what is melted, when, from what, who works the glass and what colour a melt is fit for. By custom she chooses the colours and judges every melt by eye. The last two Glassmasters each named who would follow them. Nothing says it must be so, and nothing says who may object.
- **The tally keeper** (Aldous Quill) keeps **the ledger** in the Counting House: who owes whom, what went into the Store and what came out, what the Counting House has paid. He opens the tally room from nine to three.
- **Magda Tarn** divides the morning's catch on the Fish Steps, by an old count of who fished, who has mouths to feed and who is owed.
- **Tamsin Hollin**, the glazier, fits the panes on the Ladder, so in practice she decides which glasshouse is mended first.
- **Oswin Greave** is head grower: he says what is planted where on the Ladder.
- **Marit Sedge** is warden of the Hanger, by an agreement made when it was replanted. She does not let anyone fell a tree.
- **Anselm Faro** keeps the Watchglass, under the vow (below).
- **Hobb Fenwick** keeps the cistern.
- **Mirren Quill** is the only healer and midwife. **Benedikt Orme** is the only reader and the only teacher.

## Customs

- **Drops.** The island's money is small glass tokens called drops, cast at the Cone in the Counting House's own mould. A drop is worth about one smoked fish. People keep drops at home and know roughly how many they have; prices, wages and debts are paid in them or in goods, as agreed. A casting of drops is made only when the tally keeper asks the Glassmaster for one, and is written in the ledger.
- **The ledger.** Whatever goes into or comes out of the Store is told at the Counting House and written. So are debts people choose to have written, and losses at counters that the loser cannot pay. A written debt is a public thing: anyone may ask the tally keeper what is in the book, though few do. An unwritten debt is between the two people.
- **The catch.** Each morning the line fishers bring their catch to the Fish Steps and Magda Tarn divides it. Part goes to the Store, part to the fishers' houses, and part is sold.
- **The Batch.** Pieter Achterberg lights the common oven at 03:00. Anyone may bake their roots in it while it is hot, and most households do, so the Batch is where the town meets in the morning. Whoever uses it is expected to bring a little peat now and then.
- **The Marver** is the cookshop on Mid Row. Dolly cooks there from 11:00 to 22:00, for drops or for fish, roots and greens brought in. Strand men, and Tam Roon, spend their evenings there.
- **The wash-house wall** is warm all year, because it is the Cone's own wall. On cold evenings people sit against it and talk, and Greta Dunmore hears everything.
- **The counters game.** A board of seven squares by seven, chalked on the paving, and counters of cullet glass, one colour each side. A counter surrounded on two sides is taken. Roos Achterberg carved the town's best set and runs the game under the Counting House arcade every night. People play for drops. Aldous Quill is the best player in Cullet. Anselm Faro and Pieter Achterberg play at dawn, when both are finishing their night.
- **Marks on the doors.** Each household paints its door its own colour and keeps one thing outside it: the Tarns' row of glass floats, Ode's sill of coloured cullet, the Morrows' caged gull, the Brandts' hen coop. You can tell who lives in a house without asking.
- **Apprentices.** The Kiln families have never taken a Strand apprentice at the Cone. The custom is not written anywhere; everyone knows it. Imre Sarro, whose mother was born a Tarn, is the nearest thing to an exception, and people remember that.
- **The dead** are buried on the Crown. Each grave is marked with a slab of glass of the colour the dead person chose, or that their family chose for them. Nobody digs on the Crown except to bury.
- **The Hanger** is not cut. Fallen branches may be gathered with Marit's leave. This has held for thirty years.
- **The Lintel** is left alone. Children dare each other to touch it. Benedikt Orme sits by it in the afternoons with tablets.

### The lamp and its vow

The Watchglass is lit every night at dusk and tended till dawn, as it has been every night since the Last Sail. The first Faro keeper swore that the light would burn every night until the ships came back, and every keeper since has sworn the same when they took the lamp. Anselm Faro swore it forty years ago and has never missed a night.

The lamp burns oil, and the oil is rendered from fish: every night the Watchglass burns oil that could have been several fish eaten. The Counting House pays the Faros for the oil, by an arrangement from the ship days; it is the one payment the ledger has made every year for eighty-three years. Most people have never asked who the light is for.

### The Readers' House and the tablets

The Readers' House on the Crown holds the **ship-day tablets**: slabs of slate and fired clay cut in the formal script of the carved lintels. Most are lists of dues, cargoes and ships' names. A few are harder: words in the script that nobody now can read, marks that may be numbers, and one that seems to show the island from above. No tablet says what the Lintel is, what the Pale is, or why the ships stopped.

Benedikt Orme is the only person who reads the formal script well, and Sabine Quill is learning it. Aldous Quill reads and writes the plain hand the ledger is kept in, and so does Mirren. Hobb Fenwick writes the cistern's level on a tablet every dusk. Most people can read a sign and write their name and little more. Chidi Okoro wants his children to read; Alder Fenwick wants to be a reader.

### The Cone and the colour of the melt

Glass is not made by recipe alone. The colour and clarity of a melt depend on the ash, the sand, the cullet put in and how hot the Cone runs, and the Glassmaster judges a melt by its colour when it is drawn. Ode Pontil keeps the old colour recipes in his head; none are written down. He keeps one piece of every colour ever made at the Cone on his sill. Clear glass, like the glass of the ship days, has not been made since Corvin Hollin's grandfather made a little of it. Nobody knows exactly how he did.

## How things stand on the first morning

These are facts, not plans. Who does what about them is up to each person.

- **The peat stack is falling.** About twelve days of the Cone at full fire. The cutters want more for their peat. Marit holds the Hanger.
- **The Glassmaster is sixty-one.** There are two blowers, Corvin and Imre. Vessa has not named anyone.
- **The Ladder is short three glasshouses.** Glasshouse 6 (the Greaves'), glasshouse 11 (held by the Counting House for the town) and glasshouse 13 (the Pontils') have been broken since the storm. There is glass in the lehr house for about one of them.
- **A ship stands in the dry dock.** Bram Calloway has been building the *Second Thought* for four years, from ship-day timber, driftwood and the planks of old sheds. The hull is framed. To finish it he needs fourteen trees, and the only trees are the Hanger's.
- **The Watchglass burns every night.**
- **Lark Morrow saw a sail beyond the Combs last spring,** at dusk, for as long as it takes to say so. Nobody else saw it. She has said so to anyone who would listen.
- **The ledger** has debts in drops between Strand and Kiln households, and losses at counters written in it.
- **Mid Row 6 is empty, sound and unclaimed.** Two households want it.
- **The clay bank is nearly dug out.** Otto Brandt has perhaps a season's crucibles left in it. There is good clay under the Crown, where the graves are.
- **The kelp kilns** stand upwind of Low Row, and on burning days the smoke lies along it.
- **The Counting House clock** still says ten past four.
- **It is spring.** The winter just past was hard on fuel; the last of the storm damage is still unmended.

## What nobody knows

Nobody in Cullet knows these things. Characters may wonder, guess, argue or believe, but nobody knows:

- what is beyond the Pale, or whether there is anything;
- why the ships stopped coming, and whether they will come again;
- whether the Combs can be crossed, by the *Second Thought* or anything else;
- what the Lintel is, who set it, whether it opens, and what the dark glass is;
- what the hardest tablets say;
- how clear glass was made;
- whether Lark saw a sail;
- why the clock stopped at ten past four.

Nobody knows the future: who will follow Vessa, whether the peat will last, whether the ship will be finished.

## Names

Cullet's names are original and mixed, because its people came from two different pasts.

- **Kiln names** are old Cullet names, short and plain, several of them glassmakers' words: Pontil (the iron rod a gather of glass is worked on), Frit (the half-melted batch), Hollin, Sarro, Quill, Brandt, Dunmore, Marl. Given names are old-fashioned: Vessa, Ode, Corvin, Tamsin, Runa, Aldous, Mirren, Halvard, Greta, Tobiah.
- **Strand names** come from every port the last ships touched: Calloway, Tarn, Okoro, Vane, Morrow, Sato, Dunlin; Bram, Ysolde, Magda, Chidi, Amara, Lorcan, Ingrid, Kenji, Ren.
- **The others** have names of their own trades or places: Greave, Fenwick, Roon, Sedge, Faro, Achterberg, Orme, Stannard.
- **Children** often get short names: Pim, Kit, Nell, Dory, Wick, Posy, Tilly.
- **Words everyone uses.** *Cullet*: broken glass kept for remelting, and the town. *Marver*: the slab glass is rolled on, and the cookshop. *Lehr*: the cooling oven. *Batch*: the raw mix of ash and sand, and the common oven. *A gather*: a glob of molten glass on the pipe. *Drops*: the money. *The wrack*: seaweed on the beach, and the island. *Wynds*: the stair lanes. *The Pale*, *the Combs*, *the Lintel*, *the Last Sail*.
- People say "since the Last Sail" for the date, "by the Cone" for anything certain ("it's as sure as the Cone"), and "gone to cullet" for anything broken past mending.

## Guidance for character sheets

- People are individuals first: temperament, private hopes and fears, small habits, likes and grudges. Their trade, row and family shape them; they do not define them.
- Every adult has a want beyond staying alive (the cast's `wants`), something going on at the start (`going_on`), and a usual day (`rhythm`). The sheet must agree with all three. A rhythm is a starting habit, not a rule.
- Relationships are specific and two-sided, with reasons in the person's own words: family, neighbours, the people they work beside, debts, loves, old slights, teachers and apprentices, and the line between Kiln and Strand where it matters to them.
- Memories are concrete events from their own lives: the fever winter, last autumn's storm, a melt that came out wrong, a catch, a game of counters, a wedding, the day they first worked the Cone or the weirs. The elders remember their parents' stories of the lean years; nobody remembers the ships.
- Stances are beliefs they act on (`the_lamp_must_burn`, `the_hanger_must_not_be_cut`, `kiln_and_strand_are_one_town`, `the_ship_is_a_waste_of_timber`, `the_ledger_is_fair`).
- Secrets are rare and specific. A secret appears only on the sheets of the people the cast says know it. Others in the same house do not know it.
- Children know little; their sheets are simple and centred on family, lessons and play.
- Nobody knows the future. Nobody's goal is a script. Several people's goals conflict.
